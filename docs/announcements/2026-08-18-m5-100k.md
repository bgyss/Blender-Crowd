## Claim discipline for this round

The M5 contract requires any public headline to state the tier mix, hardware,
rates, quality limitations, cache size, and render path. Every draft below
carries that. In particular, these four must survive editing:

1. **Not real time.** 13.696 ticks/s against a 30 tick/s scene is ~0.46x real
   time. 100K is a bake-and-cache workflow. No "real-time crowd" phrasing.
2. **The render evidence is about object expansion, not a money shot.** One
   scene object carries all 100,000 agents as point data. At the frame
   inspected, 1,200 agents were present and evaluated as 1,200 procedural
   instances. **Do not imply 100,000 characters drawn in one frame.**
3. **The pass turns on one recalibrated threshold.** Say so. It is the most
   scrutinable thing in the release and burying it would be the one move that
   makes the rest untrustworthy.
4. **Still one machine, one OS, one Blender version, procedural proxies.** No
   production-rig, cross-platform, or 1M claim.

## Reddit

Target subreddits: **r/blender** (visual-first, short), **r/rust** (determinism
and the measurement-bug angle land best here), **r/gamedev** or
**r/proceduralgeneration** (systems detail welcome). Read each subreddit's
self-promotion rules first. The body below is written for r/rust; for r/blender
lead with the render path and cut the statistics section.

**Title (r/rust):** I shipped a 100,000-agent crowd simulator in Rust — and the
gate failure that blocked it turned out to be three bugs in my metrics, not my
simulator

**Title (r/blender):** Blender Crowd — 100,000 agents simulated in Rust, baked
to a cache, played back through Geometry Nodes with no live sim running

**Body:**

I've been building a Blender-native crowd simulation platform and just accepted
the 100,000-agent milestone. This is my first post about it here.

The architecture is a hard split: a deterministic Rust kernel owns simulation
and bakes to a versioned cache; Blender owns authoring, layout, and rendering;
Geometry Nodes is a presentation layer and never the simulator. Playback runs
from a completed cache with no simulation session in the process at all.

**What the 100K gate proves**, on one Apple M1 Max (64 GiB, macOS aarch64,
Blender 5.2 LTS):

- 100,000 of 100,000 agents reach their destination
- Declared tier mix: 10,029 individually-stepped S1/R1 and 89,971 background
  S2/R2 on a staggered 2-tick perception and steering cadence
- 13.696 ticks/s, against a checked-in budget of 10
- 0.67 GiB of cache for 120 frames (5.7 MiB/frame), f32, 0.0 m position error,
  with cancellation recovery tested
- Every per-tier quality limit — contact rate, contact severity, stall rate,
  oscillation — met with 1.7x to 3.0x margin
- The whole population lives as point data on **one** Blender scene object; it
  is never expanded into per-agent objects

**The part I'd rather tell you than have you find.** The first 100K run failed
four quality checks. I spent the debugging assuming the simulator was wrong. It
wasn't — three of the four were defects in how I was measuring:

1. Two gated metrics were not scale-invariant. `stalled_agent_share` is a
   lifetime cumulative probability, so it climbs toward 1.0 as routes get
   longer, and routes grow with √population in this fixture. `max_penetration_depth`
   is an extremum over samples, and 100K draws 32x the samples of 10K. Both rise
   with population at *identical* solver behaviour. Fitting a constant blocking
   rate per metre to the 10K data predicted 0.283 at 100K; the run measured
   0.258 — better than constant-rate, while "failing" a 0.20 bar.

2. Background-tier contact was undercounted by exactly 2x. Agents on the 2-tick
   perception cadence get an empty neighbour list on skipped ticks —
   indistinguishable, in the code, from "checked, and nobody's there". The
   metrics read that list every tick and counted every tick as exposure. So a
   background agent could be deeply overlapping and register nothing, while
   still counting the tick as clean. The fingerprint that caught it: the same
   S1↔S2 collisions were seen 65 times from one side and 35 from the other.

3. A threshold I'd set myself was calibrated on a sample size of **one**
   observed contact (with zero at the next scale down). That isn't a
   calibration, and it's the same error class as the two metrics above.

The fix the earlier analysis proposed — a floor on the crowding-slowdown term,
to stop jams from persisting — was also wrong, and measuring it is what showed
that. The fixture is sparse at every scale: max 6 neighbours within personal
space at 10K, and **4** at 100K. There are no dense clusters to dissolve. Where
the floor did bind, on a genuinely dense scene, it made jams 74% *longer*. It
ships defaulted off, with the evidence in the code comment.

**Limitations, stated up front:**

- **Not real time.** 13.696 ticks/s against a 30 tick/s scene is ~0.46x real
  time — 2h53m of compute for 79 minutes of simulated crowd. Bake-and-cache, not
  interactive.
- **The render evidence is narrower than "100K on screen".** It proves the
  population isn't expanded into per-agent scene objects. The scene emits over
  time, so 1,200 agents were present at the frame inspected and evaluated as
  1,200 procedural instances. I'm not claiming 100,000 characters drawn in one
  frame.
- **The pass turns on one recalibrated threshold** — item 3 above. Under the old
  numbers this run fails exactly that check and passes the rest. I recalibrated
  it because it was breached at 40K too, on 65 contacts versus the single
  contact it came from, so it was unsound before this run existed. But
  "recalibrate, then pass" is a shape that deserves scrutiny, so it's in the
  report and the commit rather than left for someone to notice.
- One machine, one OS, one Blender version. Procedural proxies, not production
  rigs. No 1M claim.
- **A residual trend I can't explain**: contact rates rise ~1.74x from 40K to
  100K, equally for both tiers. That points at fixture geometry — agents per
  lane grow with √population while density is held constant — but I haven't
  proven a mechanism, so a 1M run would need its own calibration.
- M5's artist/UI task hasn't been run, so this is functionally accepted, not
  operator-validated.

Everything's public: the engineering contract, the acceptance criteria I had to
pass, the failed reports as well as the passing ones, and the limitations lists.

github.com/bgyss/Blender-Crowd

Happy to talk about the determinism model, the cache format, why Geometry Nodes
deliberately isn't the simulator, or the metric-design problem — picking
quantities that mean the same thing at 1K and at 100K turned out to be the
genuinely hard part of this milestone.

## LinkedIn

Blender Crowd now simulates **100,000 agents**, with every agent reaching its
destination. That's the milestone I set as the hard one, and it's accepted.

The measured result, on one Apple M1 Max (64 GiB, macOS, Blender 5.2 LTS):

• 100,000 of 100,000 agents arrive
• A declared tier mix: 10,029 individually-stepped agents and 89,971 background
  agents on a 2-tick perception and steering cadence
• 13.696 simulation ticks/s
• 0.67 GiB of cache for 120 frames, at 0.0 m position error
• Every per-tier quality limit met with 1.7x–3.0x margin
• The whole population stays as point data on one Blender scene object, never
  expanded into 100,000 objects

Two things that number is not. It is **not real time** — 13.696 ticks/s against
a 30 tick/s scene is about 0.46x, so this is a bake-and-cache pipeline. And the
render evidence proves the population isn't expanded into per-agent objects; it
does not show 100,000 characters in a single frame.

The engineering story is more useful than the headline.

The first 100K attempt failed four quality checks. I assumed the simulator had a
scaling problem and spent the investigation looking for it. **Three of the four
failures were defects in my measurements, and none were in the simulator.**

Two of the gated metrics weren't scale-invariant: they rise with population even
when behaviour is byte-for-byte identical. One was a lifetime cumulative
probability, the other an extremum over a sample count that grew 32x. A third
was worse — background-tier contact was undercounted exactly 2x, because agents
on a sparse perception cadence couldn't be *seen* colliding on skipped ticks
while those ticks still counted as clean exposure.

And the fix I'd proposed for the remainder — a floor on the crowding term —
turned out to be wrong when I measured it. The crowd is sparse at every scale.
Where the floor did bind, it made jams 74% longer. It ships disabled.

The lesson I'd take to any performance work: **when a metric moves at scale,
establish that the metric means the same thing at both scales before you go
looking for the regression.** A rate that isn't actually a rate will manufacture
a phantom regression, and you can spend a long time optimising against it.

One more thing worth saying out loud: the final pass turns on a single
recalibrated threshold — one I'd originally derived from a sample size of one
observed contact. I recalibrated it because it was independently breached at a
smaller scale, but "recalibrate, then pass" deserves scrutiny, so it's stated in
the report and the commit message rather than left to be discovered.

The contract, the acceptance criteria, the failed reports alongside the passing
ones, and the full limitations list are all public.

github.com/bgyss/Blender-Crowd
